\section{Methodology}

The identification of use cases for the \textit{TenSyGrid} Toolbox follows a user-centred design approach and considers the different roles involved in stability studies. The catalogue was derived from four complementary sources:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Project objectives.} The TenSyGrid proposal defines the toolbox as a stability-assessment platform for converter-dominated power grids. Its core workflow consists of loading a grid model, running a small-signal stability analysis, and interpreting the results.
  \item \textbf{Review of existing tools.} The research group's experience with established stability-analysis software was used to identify recurring study tasks, expected outputs, and limitations that a new toolbox should address.
  \item \textbf{Prototype and internal technical feedback.} An initial web-based Stability Monitor implements five core interaction flows: analysis execution (UC-01), system-status monitoring and eigenvalue analysis (UC-02), critical-mode inspection (UC-03), bus-voltage evaluation (UC-04), and $N-1$ contingency assessment (UC-05). Feedback gathered while developing the prototype was used to formalise these implemented use cases and identify candidate extensions.
  \item \textbf{Industry interviews.} Semi-structured interviews with transmission-system, consulting, and equipment-manufacturing stakeholders were used to validate the relevance of the implemented workflows and formulate additional needs. These needs informed the proposed use cases UC-06 to UC-14, including scenario comparison, trend monitoring, device attribution, and advanced nonlinear assessment.
\end{enumerate}

Each use case is described following a structured template that includes \textbf{identifier} and \textbf{name}, \textbf{actor}, \textbf{objective}, \textbf{preconditions}, \textbf{theoretical basis}, \textbf{possible bottlenecks}, \textbf{main flow}, \textbf{GUI interaction}, and \textbf{expected result}.

The toolbox interface follows Shneiderman's visual-information-seeking approach: \textit{overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand}~\cite{shneiderman1996eyes}. In practical terms, the user can:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item obtain an overview of the system status and its principal stability indicators;
  \item zoom into critical modes and regions of the eigenvalue spectrum;
  \item filter and sort the results to focus on the most relevant dynamics; and
  \item request details on demand, including modal metrics, participation factors, and the physical devices associated with each mode.
\end{enumerate}

This interaction sequence supports both rapid operational screening and deeper technical diagnosis. Once the relevant modes and devices have been identified, the resulting evidence can be used in subsequent use cases to formulate recommendations for controller retuning, contingency mitigation, or further studies.

\section{Tool scheme and structure}

\subsection{Initial tool scheme and architectural evolution}

\paragraph{Initial conceptual scheme (Figure~\ref{fig:tool_scheme}).}
At the outset of the TenSyGrid project, the preliminary architectural roadmap considered MATLAB as a key foundation of the development platform. This initial strategy was primarily motivated by the pre-existing research legacy within the consortium, specifically the Multilinear Time-Invariant (MTI) Toolbox developed in earlier and concurrent project stages~\cite{Kaufmann2025TenSyGridD11,Wong2025TenSyGridD21}, alongside the STAMP simulation framework. In this early phase, before key design decisions regarding runtime dependencies were finalized, the toolbox was conceived as a hybrid, multi-branch framework connecting open-source components with existing MATLAB prototypes through dedicated runtime adapters.

This initial conceptual architecture is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:tool_scheme}. As originally planned, the platform established two parallel simulation branches:
\begin{enumerate}[before=\leavevmode, leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{VeraGrid Platform (Python):} Designed as an open-source modeling environment incorporating modular calculation engines for power flow ($\text{VG}_\text{PF}$), dynamic root-mean-square transient simulations ($\text{VG}_\text{RMS}$), small-signal stability analysis ($\text{VG}_\text{SSig}$), and a dedicated multilinear/tensor integration layer ($\text{VG}_\text{MTI}$).
  \item \textbf{STAMP and MTI Framework (MATLAB):} Encapsulating the original MTI tensor formulation and STAMP power-flow ($\text{S}_\text{PF}$), electromagnetic transient ($\text{S}_\text{EMT}$), and small-signal ($\text{S}_\text{SSig}$) routines, intended to interact with Python via bi-directional process adapters.
  \item \textbf{Interoperability Layer (IPSA):} Envisioned to ingest network topologies and dynamic controller parameters from external industry-standard tools (PSCAD, DIgSILENT PowerFactory, PSS/E, MATLAB/Simulink).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Strategic architectural consolidation within VeraGrid.}
While this initial hybrid layout enabled rapid early prototyping by leveraging existing research codebases, detailed evaluation during the architectural definition phase showed that maintaining multiple distinct software packages and heterogeneous runtimes introduced significant operational and maintenance complexity. To establish a production-grade, highly cohesive toolbox for Transmission System Operators (TSOs), the consortium identified substantial advantages in consolidating the entire calculation pipeline into a unified, native Python framework centered around VeraGrid:
\begin{itemize}[before=\leavevmode, leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Single-Stack Architectural Simplicity and Cohesion:} Interfacing separate runtimes across language boundaries required dedicated inter-process communication (IPC) bridges, serialization adapters, and dual-environment management. Consolidating the core multilinear routines natively within VeraGrid's architecture removes multi-package glue code, eliminates data marshaling overhead, and ensures unified memory structures directly shared between the power flow, descriptor assembly, and eigenvalue solvers.
  \item \textbf{Direct VeraGrid Integration and Codebase Maintainability:} Embedding the multilinear formulation directly into the \texttt{VeraGridEngine} ecosystem leverages VeraGrid's native high-performance data model and compiled extensions (Python). This streamlines continuous integration, simplifies testing, and provides a single, uniform API for developers, researchers, and operators alike.
  \item \textbf{Automated Batch Studies:} A unified Python and VeraGrid environment supports repeated studies, such as $N-1$ contingency evaluations and parameter sweeps, through a common codebase. This reduces configuration and maintenance effort across the analysis workflow.
  \item \textbf{Open-Source Accessibility and Reproducibility:} A primary pillar of the European CETPartnership initiative is the creation of openly accessible, transparent, and easily reproducible tools for the broader power systems community. Delivering a fully open-source Python codebase enables TSOs, research institutions, and industry stakeholders to readily inspect, customize, and extend the algorithms without prerequisite software barriers.
  \item \textbf{Seamless Web and Modern Service Coupling:} Python's extensive ecosystem natively bridges scientific computing libraries (NumPy, SciPy, optimized sparse solvers) with modern asynchronous web frameworks (such as FastAPI). This architecture enables high-throughput data streaming to the operator-facing browser interface with minimal overhead.
\end{itemize}

Consequently, the consortium agreed to consolidate the computational core natively within Python and VeraGrid. The multilinear algorithms and tensor formulations of the MTI framework were re-engineered directly into the dedicated \texttt{TensyGridEngine}, running seamlessly as an integrated calculation layer built directly on top of \texttt{VeraGridEngine}.

All downstream analyses operate on VeraGrid's internal \texttt{MultiCircuit} representation and are therefore independent of the original input channel. A model may be loaded directly from any format supported by VeraGrid, obtained through the IPSA integration layer from tools such as PSCAD, DIgSILENT PowerFactory, PSS/E, or MATLAB/Simulink, or supplied through a configured API endpoint. In every case, the input must provide the topology, equipment parameters, operating point, and dynamic-model information required by the selected analysis and must pass the same conversion and validation process before execution.

The modular decoupling illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:tool_pipeline} ensures clear separation of concerns across the pipeline:
\begin{enumerate}[before=\leavevmode, leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Ingestion and standardisation:} Downstream computational modules remain completely agnostic to the proprietary origins of the network models, relying exclusively on standard internal interfaces.
  \item \textbf{High-performance core computing:} Heavy numerical operations (power flow, descriptor matrix assembly, generalized eigenvalue solves, and batch contingency runs) run natively in optimized Python libraries on dedicated servers or HPC compute nodes.
  \item \textbf{Browser-based operator access:} Transmission operators access results through an intuitive, lightweight web browser interface without needing local numerical software or specialized compiler toolchains installed on control-room workstations.
\end{enumerate}



\section{Operator interviews}
To ground the use-case catalogue in real operational practice, three semi-structured interviews were conducted with transmission-system stakeholders; the key findings that shaped the use cases are summarised below.

\paragraph{Former TSO planner and control-room analyst in South America.} The interview described an operational workflow based on economic dispatch coordinated in 8-hour shifts, system validation every 15--30 minutes, and \mbox{$N-1$} contingency screening complemented by short-circuit and RMS dynamic simulations for critical cases. On the planning side, detailed modelling of low-voltage networks and IBRs (including protection systems) overloaded the state-space representation, making small-signal studies (SSTI, SSSI, SSOS) take 3--4 hours. Key operational metrics were the frequency nadir, ROCOF, and the settling time $T_{1090}$; in planning, eigenvalue position, damping, SCR variation, and participation factors were used to avoid out-of-band interactions. Regarding the GUI prototype, the interviewee recommended: (i) simplifying status indicators while keeping colour coding in the complex plane; (ii) providing explicit error diagnostics when an analysis fails; (iii) linking eigenvalues and participation factors to specific devices and state variables ($\omega$) to distinguish local from inter-area oscillations; (iv) adding visualisation of operational bands (P-V curves); and (v) supporting sequential contingencies (chains of events, $N$-$2$) and multiple simultaneous contingency selection (e.g.\ via YAML configuration).

\paragraph{HVDC project leader at an equipment manufacturer.} The interview outlined three families of studies performed in the HVDC context: (i) \emph{dynamic performance} of an HVDC link under grid events, run in EMT with hundreds of PSCAD cases over 1-2 days; (ii) \emph{RMS} whole-country contingency screening ($N$-$1$) on AC networks of the order of 500\,km radius (e.g.\ the UK transmission network), taking a few hours; and (iii) \emph{screening studies} to identify synchronous generators that may interact problematically with the HVDC, run as load-flow and short-circuit analyses in minutes. Small-signal studies are currently performed through impedance scanning (SS-SFI), with the frequency response obtained from PSCAD; clients are beginning to request state-space models (grey-box, per subsystem) for which a Laplace transfer function may be delivered instead, keeping the full model confidential for security reasons. The interview also highlighted harmonic-stability assessment based on gain/phase margins rather than eigenvalues, the need to study voltage collapse in weak grids, and the value of automating time-domain study campaigns and result post-processing.

\paragraph{European mainland TSO specialist.} The interview described long-term grid development, short-term planning, and operational processes from week-ahead down to intraday and real-time operation (SCADA/EMS with state estimation). Models are exchanged in CGMES; PowerFactory, PSCAD, and SCADA are the three main software tools. Dynamic security assessment (DSA) involves thousands of contingency cases; computation time limits model detail, and the trade-off is managed by increasing the number of cores. Day-ahead studies use RMS models, while close-to-real-time studies run about 100 cases with reduced detail. Outage management is not automatic, and there is interest in decision-support tools for judging criticality. Regarding HVDC, the interviewee noted the move from Thevenin equivalents towards reduced models and stressed that any deployed tool must integrate with the existing commercial software stack. The completeness of CGMES for dynamic data and the value of software-independent EMT models were also discussed, with VeraGrid identified as a relevant integration platform.

The evidence from the interviews was separated into validation of existing work and requirements for further development:

\begin{itemize}[before=\leavevmode, leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Implemented workflows validated by the interviews.} The stakeholders confirmed the relevance of loading representative operating points (UC-01), inspecting eigenvalues and damping (UC-02), linking critical modes to state variables and physical devices (UC-03), checking operating-point quantities (UC-04), and screening $N-1$ contingencies (UC-05). They also identified improvements required in these workflows, notably clearer error diagnostics, device-level attribution, operational bands, and support for multiple or sequential contingencies.
  \item \textbf{Additional requirements identified through the interviews.} Scenario comparison and stability-margin tracking motivated UC-06 and UC-08; device attribution and systematic contingency ranking motivated UC-07 and UC-09; actionable mitigation guidance motivated UC-10; converter-penetration studies motivated UC-11; and repeatable documentation motivated UC-12. The weak-grid and large-disturbance questions provided additional motivation for the research-oriented UC-13 and UC-14.
\end{itemize}

The interviews therefore validate the direction of the five implemented use cases while providing evidence for, rather than a commitment to implement, the nine proposed extensions.
